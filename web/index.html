Understanding the Data

What does IGNI measure?

IGNI analyzes satellite-detected active-fire activity. Hotspot detections are not necessarily distinct physical fires and do not directly measure burned area.

What does harmonized mean?

MODIS and VIIRS have different detection capabilities. IGNI uses MODIS-derived VIIRS-equivalent historical estimates alongside observed VIIRS data to support comparisons across time.

How screening indicators are assigned?

    Rules are evaluated in the order shown. These are application-defined historical screening heuristics, not scientifically validated wildfire-risk or emergency-response thresholds. Only complete, comparable periods are classified. Hotspot detections are not distinct fires or burned area; zero detections do not prove that no physical fires occurred.

    What the numbers are

    Each number counts satellite hotspot detections: pixels where MODIS or VIIRS saw a thermal anomaly during an overpass. One fire can produce many detections, so counts measure burning activity, not the number of fires or the area burned.

    All counts are in VIIRS-equivalent units. VIIRS (375 m pixels) records several times more hotspots than MODIS (1 km pixels) over the same fires, so the older MODIS-only years are converted to what VIIRS S-NPP would have counted.

    1. Collect. MODIS Terra and Aqua (2003 onward) and VIIRS S-NPP (2012 onward) detections over Indonesia from NASA FIRMS, archive and near-real-time.
    2. Clean. Keep nominal and high confidence detections, then flag volcanoes, gas flares and other static heat sources so only vegetation fires are counted.
    3. Harmonize. A model trained on 2012 to 2026, when both sensors flew, converts MODIS counts in each 0.25° cell to VIIRS-equivalent counts for 2003 to January 2012.

    How accurate the model years are

    Leave-one-year-out test: each year from 2012 to 2026 is hidden, the model is trained on the rest, and its estimate is compared with the real VIIRS total.

    Interpretation and limitations

    Detection is not proof of a distinct fire. Hotspot counts measure satellite-detected thermal activity. Multiple detections may come from one physical fire, and zero detections do not prove that no physical fires occurred.

    Harmonized values support historical comparability. Model-derived values before the documented transition are estimates, while later values are observed VIIRS S-NPP counts. Leave-one-year-out validation summarizes model performance over annual and monthly aggregates; its errors are not universal cell-level confidence intervals.

    Provincial activity is geographically assigned. The dashboard assigns each 0.25° grid cell to a province using its centre, with the documented nearest-province treatment for offshore centres.

    Priority Watchlist categories are screening indicators. They are application-defined comparisons with each province’s own historical record, not validated emergency-response classifications or wildfire-danger levels.

    Background

    The physics behind it

    Three ideas behind the numbers: why two satellites count fires differently, why some fires make far more smoke, and when that smoke reaches Singapore and Kuala Lumpur.

    Why MODIS and VIIRS disagree

    A MODIS pixel is about 1 km wide. A VIIRS pixel is 375 m wide, about 7 times smaller in area, so the same small fire fills a bigger share of it and is easier to detect.

    VIIRS therefore records about 4.5 to 6 times more detections than MODIS (approx., it changes from year to year). That is why the two records are harmonized before they are compared.

    From fire to haze

    Many fires in Sumatra and Kalimantan burn peat, a thick layer of old, partly decayed plants. Peat smoulders without flames, at low temperature and for weeks, sometimes underground.

    Smouldering makes a lot of fine smoke (PM2.5), so one hotspot over peat can mean far more haze than one over grass.

    The monsoon decides where smoke goes

    • Southwest monsoon, approx. June to October
    • Northeast monsoon, approx. November to March

    From about June to October the southwest monsoon carries smoke from Sumatra across the Strait of Malacca to Singapore and Kuala Lumpur. From about November to March the northeast monsoon brings rain and turns the wind, so smoke moves away.

    In dry years the effect is strongest: in 2015 (strong El Niño) and 2019 (positive Indian Ocean Dipole) haze reached both cities for weeks.
